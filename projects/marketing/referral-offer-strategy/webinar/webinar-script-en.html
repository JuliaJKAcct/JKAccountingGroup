2 · Open the coffee shop

0:06–0:13Slides 5–6◆ Coffee shop

On the slide: the leaky bucket — revenue pours in, drains out through holes.

What to say

"Let's build a business together — and I'll keep coming back to it all webinar. A coffee shop. Things are going well, and you decide to grow: you put $30,000 into growth — a new location, more people, more volume. It works — sales double. You celebrate.

Three months later you look at the account — and the money's gone. Just like Shop B. Not because you sold too little. Because growth amplified what was already leaking. Picture a bucket: revenue pours in from the top, growth makes the stream stronger. But if the bucket has holes — the stronger the stream, the faster it drains."

2

Belief shift #2

You don't have a sales problem. You have a profit-leak problem.

Engage the room

"Raise your hand if any of this has ever happened:"

  • Record sales — but making payroll was a stretch.
  • You had to delay paying yourself to cover the month.
  • You put the business on a credit card to bridge to customer payments.

Live — hands (half the room will raise). Recorded — "type YES in the chat / admit it to yourself." Then: "That's the leaks. Today we find where they come from."

Co-host (Boris)

If you're co-hosting with Boris — this is his 3–4 minutes: why these owners' growth took off (demand, channels) and why growth is exactly what exposes the weak spots in the finances. The logic: "Boris helped them grow — I'll show where the money leaks out."

3 · Leak 1: the margin you never saw

0:13–0:24Slides 7–8◆ Coffee shop

What to say

"A $5,000 corporate order comes into the shop. Great day, right? Let's subtract everything — honestly."

Order$5,000
  • Ingredients & product (≈28%)− $1,400
  • Staff to make & deliver (≈18%)− $900
  • Rent & utilities, share (≈12%)− $600
  • Your time (≈8%)− $400
  • Insurance, software, supplies (≈7%)− $350
  • Taxes on the profit (est.)− $320
  • Other operating costs (≈4%)− $200
  • Card processing (≈3%)− $150
You keep$680

What to say

"Out of five thousand, six hundred and eighty is left. Not five thousand. Six hundred and eighty. And that's a generous version. I see this in real books all the time: the owner is sure they made five thousand, and less than seven hundred lands in the account."

Engage the room — they calculate it themselves

"Now your turn. Grab your phone, open the calculator. Think of your last big order or sale. Subtract along with me: cost of goods… payroll… rent… fees… taxes… your own time. Don't rush — I'll wait."

Hold a 30–40 second pause and actually let them do the math. Live — "call out what's left in the chat." Recorded — "write down your number, you'll want it later." When someone gets the number themselves — it's theirs.

Slide 8 (teaching): the margin formula, applied to the shop.

What to say (give the formula)

"Now let's name it. What we just calculated is called margin — what's actually left, not revenue. The formula is simple, write it down:

Margin = (Revenue − All costs) ÷ Revenue × 100%

For our shop: 680 divided by 5,000 is 13.6%. A healthy margin is roughly 10–15%. Under five is an alarm — even while revenue is growing. Take your own number from the calculator, divide it by the order total — that's your real margin. Remember it, you'll need it again today."

This is your main "aha." The pause after "$680," and the pause for their own math, matter more than any words. The numbers are realistic: coffee shops net 5–12%, so "$680 on $5,000" is on the generous side.

4 · Leak 2: the money that grows against you

0:24–0:31Slides 9–10◆ Coffee shop

What to say

"The second leak is time. Before it grew, the shop bought beans, hired staff, paid for ads — the money went out today. The revenue for it arrives weeks later. While the business is small, the gap is bearable. But as you grow, the gap grows with you — and here's the paradox: sales up, and the account empty. That's how growing companies break — not from too few sales, but from running out of cash in the moment."

Engage the room — the question that sticks

"Answer honestly: how many months could your business survive if every customer paid 30 days late?"

Give it a pause. Live — hands: "who's more than three months? who's under one?" Recorded — "say the number out loud to yourself." For many, the answer is "less than I thought."

Slide 10 (teaching): the cash-runway formula.

What to say (give the formula)

"That feeling turns into one number — runway. Here it is:

Runway (months) = Cash on hand ÷ Average monthly spend

If you have $30,000 in the account and spend $20,000 a month, your runway is a month and a half. Comfortable is three months or more. Under one, and the business lives paycheck to paycheck — and growth only makes it sharper. Profit shows up on paper; runway shows up in months, and it's what tells you whether you'd survive a bad quarter."

5 · Leak 3: you're driving by the rear-view mirror

0:31–0:38Slides 11–13◆ Coffee shop
3

Belief shift #3

Accounting isn't about taxes. It's about timely decisions.

Engage the room

"Simple question. Who can name their profit from last month — right now, from memory? Raise your hand."

Usually about 10% of hands. Recorded — "honestly: can you or not?"

What to say

"Exactly. Almost no one. And look: the shop didn't notice its margin sliding — the books were three months behind. By the time the numbers were in, the money had already leaked out. That's driving by the rear-view mirror. You don't need accounting for the tax office — you need it to make a decision in time, while you can still change something."

Slide 13 (teaching): the monthly close and the 3 numbers.

What to say (give the standard)

"So what does 'right' look like? Proper bookkeeping closes the month: by the 10th–15th you have last month's profit-and-loss in hand. Not a quarter later — two weeks later. And each month you only need to watch three numbers:

1) Revenue — what came in 2) Margin — what's left 3) Cash on hand — how long it lasts

That's your dashboard. Three numbers, once a month, on time — and the 'hire / raise the price / slow down' decision gets made while the money is still there, not after it's leaked out."

6 · Leak 4: the structure quietly taxing you

0:38–0:45Slides 14–16◆ Coffee shop

What to say

"The fourth leak is structure. While the shop grew, the owner just pulled money out as they went. The entity stayed the same as day one. The S-corp election was never filed in time. Sales tax in a new state — never registered. And then a letter from the IRS.

Nothing exotic — an ordinary story. But each of these little things becomes real money as you grow: thousands of dollars a year in taxes alone. Growth didn't create these problems — it amplified them."

Slide 15 (teaching): S-corp vs LLC in real dollars.

What to say (compare LLC and S-corp)

"Let me show you in numbers — and, importantly, what we're comparing against. The business makes $100,000 in profit a year. As a plain LLC, self-employment tax — Social Security and Medicare, that 15.3% — applies to all of the profit. On $100k, that's $15,300 a year.

Now the S-corp. You pay yourself a reasonable salary — say $50,000. The same 15.3% applies only to the salary. The other ~$50,000 you take as a distribution — and that tax doesn't apply to it at all.

LLC: 15.3% × $100k = $15,300 · S-corp: 15.3% × $50k = $7,650

$15,300 versus $7,650 — half the tax. That's about $7,650 that stays in your pocket, every year, for one right decision about structure."

Three rules for the whole webinar

  • Let them discover first — then give the formula. Every 5–7 minutes, someone should think "oh… that's me" — and only then get one short concept to measure it. Formula after the pain, not instead of it.
  • Everything loops back to the coffee shop. One story, not four separate topics — that's how it sticks.
  • Advisor's voice, not a lecturer's. "I've seen this across dozens of owners — here's what happens next." That's your edge over everyone who just explains taxes.
