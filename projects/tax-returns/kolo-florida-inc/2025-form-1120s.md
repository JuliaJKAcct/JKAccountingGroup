⛔ **Three credit lines, one per asset — never one lump.** The chart carries an accumulated-
depreciation account per asset, and **Schedule L line 10b, each asset's net book value, and the
gain or loss on disposal are all built from them.** Lump them and the year an asset leaves you
cannot say what its basis was. *(This is also exactly how the 2024 entry was posted.)*

#### ❓ "Why is the asset account not in this entry?" — the question, and the answer

**The natural expectation is that a depreciation entry touches the asset accounts** — `Computer
Equipment`, `Floor Scrubber`, `Furniture`. **It must not, and the reason is the whole design of
fixed-asset accounting.**

🔑 **The asset account holds ORIGINAL COST and never moves again.** It is set when the asset is
bought and it stays at 6,753 / 2,261 / 9,306 until the day the asset **leaves** the company. It is
a historical fact, not a current value.

**Depreciation is recorded in a second account that sits beside it — a *contra-asset*.** It
accumulates, year after year, everything written off so far. The two are read **as a pair**:

```
  Computer Equipment                      6,753   ← cost. NEVER changes.
  Accumulated Depreciation — Computer    (3,174)  ← the contra. Grows every year.
                                        ───────
  Net book value                          3,579   ← what the balance sheet shows
```

**Why not just credit the asset down to 3,579 directly and skip the second account?** Because you
would destroy the only two facts anyone later needs:

| If you credit the ASSET | If you credit ACCUMULATED DEPRECIATION |
|---|---|
| Cost is lost — after a few years nobody can say what was paid | Cost survives, permanently |
| You cannot tell a **cheap old asset** from an **expensive nearly-written-off one** — both show a small number | The pair tells you instantly: 9,306 cost with 3,213 taken is a different animal from a 3,213 asset |
| **Schedule L cannot be filled in.** Line **10a** wants *cost* and line **10b** wants *accumulated depreciation* — as two separate figures | Both lines read straight off the two accounts |
| Gain or loss on sale is unprovable, because gain = proceeds − (cost − accumulated) | Computable at any moment |

🛑 **Schedule L is the concrete proof.** Look at what the filed return needs:

```
10a  Buildings and other depreciable assets ......  18,320   ← the three ASSET accounts
 b   Less accumulated depreciation .............. ( 7,167)   ← the three CONTRA accounts
                                                  ───────
                                                   11,153
```

**The form asks for both numbers separately.** If depreciation had been credited against the asset
accounts, line 10a would read 11,153, line 10b would be blank, and the return could not be prepared
as the form is designed.

ⓘ **So the entry has exactly two kinds of line, and neither is an asset account:** one **debit** to
the *expense* (`Depreciation`, which flows to page 1 line 14 and reduces this year's income), and
one **credit per asset** to that asset's *accumulated depreciation* (which sits on the balance
sheet and never reverses). **The asset accounts are deliberately untouched — they are the one thing
in this picture that is supposed to stand still.**

📌 **When do the asset accounts finally move?** On **disposal** — sale, scrapping, or liquidation.
Then *both* lines are removed together: the asset is credited for its full original cost and the
accumulated depreciation is debited for everything taken. **That is 2026's entry for this client**,
and it is why the per-asset split matters so much here.

🛑 **But the books entry is NOT the tax computation, and for 2026 that difference is the whole
point.** An earlier draft of this paragraph said the gain was "the difference against any
proceeds". **For a liquidation there are no proceeds, and that reading produces a full write-off
loss the statute does not allow:**

> **IRC §336(a)**, verbatim — *"**Except as otherwise provided in this section or section 337**,
> gain or loss shall be recognized to a liquidating corporation on the distribution of property in
> complete liquidation **as if such property were sold to the distributee at its fair market
> value**."*
>
> ⓘ That opening clause is not filler — *"except as otherwise provided in this section"* is what
> carries **§336(d)**, the loss limitation set out just below.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sale** | gain = **amount realized** − adjusted basis. ⚠️ **Selling expenses go on the BASIS side** — Form 4797 line **21** is *"Cost or other basis **plus expense of sale**"* — not netted off the price |
| **Adjusted basis** | uses Form 4797 line **22**, *"Depreciation (or depletion) **allowed or allowable**"* — the **tax** figure. It equals this contra account only because the books here carry the tax depreciation; for a client with its own book depreciation it does not |
| 🛑 **Liquidation** | **§336(a) deems a sale at FAIR MARKET VALUE.** No cash arriving is not the same as no proceeds. **§336(d)(1)** can also **disallow a LOSS**, but it takes **two conditions, not one** — see below |
| Scrapping / abandonment | an ordinary loss on **Form 4797 line 10** |

#### ⚠️ §336(d)(1) — read the test properly, because relatedness alone does NOT disallow the loss

**The statute:** *no loss is recognized on a distribution to a **related person** (§267)* **if**
*(i)* **the distribution is not pro rata**, **or** *(ii)* **the property is disqualified property**
— acquired by the corporation in a **§351** transaction or as a **contribution to capital** during
the **5 years** ending on the distribution.

**So it is `related` AND (`not pro rata` OR `disqualified`).** Applied here:

| Prong | Kolo |
|---|---|
| **Related person?** | ✅ **Yes, and already settled** — a **sole 100% shareholder** is related under §267. This is not the open question |
| **Not pro rata?** | ❌ **Cannot be met.** With one shareholder owning 100%, **every** distribution is pro rata by construction |
| **Disqualified property?** | 🔎 **The only live prong.** The three assets were placed in service **after** incorporation and carry real costs, which reads as **purchased** — but confirm none came in via §351 or as a capital contribution, because this company's whole life sits inside the 5-year window |

📌 **On the evidence in this file, §336(d)(1) most likely does NOT disallow the loss** — but that is
a conclusion to reach deliberately, not to assume, and it turns on the third row alone.
*(§336(d)(2) is a separate anti-stuffing rule for property contributed with a loss-recognition
purpose; it needs the same acquisition facts.)*

#### Why the IRS percentage table does not apply here — the short tax year

ⓘ The MACRS table (Pub 946 Table A-1, year 2 = 32.00% for 5-year, 24.49% for 7-year) applied to

🔑 **Pub 946, chapter 4, "Figuring the Deduction for a Short Tax Year", first sentence, verbatim:**

> *"You cannot use the MACRS percentage tables to determine depreciation for a short tax year. A

Kolo was incorporated **30 May 2024**, so its first tax year ended 31 December without ever being

measured against at all**, and the register's **remaining-basis** computation — adjusted basis at
the start of the year × the applicable rate, which is Pub 946's own *"simplified method"* — is the
**required** method, not ATX's preference. **That is what 2025 follows and 2026 must keep.**

**So it depends on what was actually bought, not on what the bookkeeper called the account:**

| If the arrangement is… | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Storage of the client's INVENTORY** — a warehouse holds goods, the provider picks the spot, the bill moves with pallets / cubic feet / volume | ✅ **Excepted.** `storage` is on the list. **No 1099** |
| **A LEASE of identified space** — a unit or bay, a term, a fixed monthly amount, the client holds a key | 🛑 **1099-MISC box 1 rent**, reportable |
| **Predominantly FULFILMENT work** — pick/pack, kitting, returns processing, with storage incidental | ⚠️ **Not covered by anything we could verify.** The exception names *storage*, not order fulfilment. **Treat conservatively as a service → 1099-NEC** |
| **Genuinely both, separately priced** | **Prorate** — rent to 1099-MISC box 1, services to 1099-NEC |

📌 **What settles it: the signed agreement — its title, its premises clause and its fee schedule —
read with one full invoice showing how the charge is computed.** A fixed monthly amount against an
identified unit is a lease; a bill that moves with pallets, cubic feet or orders is a service.
⛔ **Neither name is evidence.** `Storage Rent` was chosen by whoever set up the chart of accounts,
and a payee name that reads like a logistics provider tells us nothing either — **`LLC` is not even
a usable signal of tax status.** Do not file a 1099 for this one, **or decline to**, on the strength
of a name.

ⓘ **This does not settle 14a on its own** — the **landlord** carries it to Yes independently,
subject only to its own W-9. What the storage fork changes is **which form** that payee gets, if
any.

**The exemptions that do the work here, and they are the ones people forget:**

1. **The test is WHO RECEIVED the money, not what was bought.** Payments to a **corporation** are
   exempt — that is what clears the accounting fees and the car rental. ⛔ **Not because they are
   "professional fees":** accounting, consulting and design fees are among the most commonly
   *reportable* payments there are; they are exempt here only because our firm is a `Corp`.
   *"Corp"/"Inc" in the name is the signal; an **LLC is not**, because an LLC may be taxed as a sole
   proprietorship, partnership **or** corporation, and only the last is exempt. **You cannot tell
   from the name — you need the W-9, **line 3a**.*** ⚠️ **And a single-member LLC correctly returns that W-9 with `Individual/sole proprietor` ticked and no LLC letter — it is a disregarded entity, and it is NOT exempt.**
   ⚠️ **And five things are reportable even to a corporation:** attorneys' fees (1099-NEC box 1) ·
   gross proceeds to an attorney (1099-MISC box 10) · **medical and health care payments (box 6)** ·
   substitute dividend payments (box 8) · fish for resale (box 11). **None applies to Kolo**, but
   the medical one is skipped constantly.
2. **RENT is a 1099 item.** 1099-**MISC box 1**, $600+, paid in the course of a trade or business.
   This is the one that gets missed, because people think "1099 = contractors". ⓘ Two carve-outs:
   rent paid to a **corporation**, and rent paid **through a real-estate agent or property
   manager** (they file, not you).
3. **Zelle does NOT relieve the payer.** Zelle is not a third-party settlement organization and
   issues no 1099-K, so unlike a card or PayPal payment, **the payer's own 1099 duty stands.**
   All the Contract Labor payments here were Zelle.

📌 **What settles 14a — a W-9 from each of the two REMAINING payees.** *(Which **form** the storage
payee gets also needs its contract, above.)* **Only if both LLCs turn out to be taxed as
corporations is 14a genuinely No.** Otherwise the return needs **14a = Yes**, and 14b answers
whether the forms were filed. ✅ The Contract Labor payee is out of this question entirely.

## 7 · Carry into 2026 — the FINAL return

🛑 **NOTHING IN THIS SECTION BLOCKS THE 2025 RETURN.** Everything below is an input to **next
year's** filing. The 2025 return is gated only by §6. **Two rows are marked NOT ESTABLISHED and
they are 2026 work, not 2025 work** — they are recorded here now only because the facts they need
(what the assets are worth, how they were acquired) get harder to reconstruct once the company is
gone and the books are unreachable.

**Why a final return needs them at all.** When a company closes, everything it still owns has to
**leave** — the inventory and the three fixed assets. There are **two routes out, and they are taxed
differently:**

| Route | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **SOLD to a third party** during the wind-down | An ordinary sale at the **actual proceeds**. ✅ **No FMV estimate is needed — the sale price IS the number** |
| **DISTRIBUTED in kind** to the shareholder | 🛑 **§336(a) treats it as if the company had SOLD it to him at fair market value**, even though no money changes hands |

**So the FMV question only bites on what is actually handed over rather than sold** — and for 11,000+
units of retail stock, **selling is at least as likely**, which would supply the figure instead of
requiring one. ⓘ Creditors are paid first either way; the balance sheet still carries **4,964** of
accounts payable.

Where §336(a) does apply, the return reports a **gain or loss per item**, `FMV − adjusted basis`:

- ✅ **The adjusted basis is the half we hold** — that is exactly what the per-asset depreciation
  split in §2 is for. ⚠️ **But not the 31-Dec-2025 figure straight off the table:** a **disposal-year
  depreciation deduction still runs first** (half a year under the half-year convention — roughly
  **1,798** of the register's 3,596 projection), and it reduces basis before the computation.
- ⛔ **The FMV is the half nobody holds.**
