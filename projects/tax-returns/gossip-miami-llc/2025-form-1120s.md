### ① What actually happened, in plain words

**An S corporation is a company whose profit is not taxed at the company — it is handed to the owners,
who put it on their own returns.** 🔑 **The law only lets certain people be those owners: US citizens
and US residents** *(plus a few kinds of trust and estate)*. **Someone who is not a US tax resident
cannot be one.**

🛑 **So the moment such a person acquired the membership interests — 30 October 2025 — the company
stopped being an S corporation.** ⚠️ **Nobody chose this and nobody filed anything.** There is no form
to sign and no election to revoke; the status simply ends on that day, under **§1362(d)(2)**.
⛔ **And it cannot be undone by preparing the return differently.**

> 🔵 **What the company became instead — and it is NOT "a normal LLC again".** Gossip Miami is an LLC.
> An LLC that elects S status is **treated as having also elected to be a CORPORATION** *(Reg.
> §301.7701-3(c)(1)(v)(C) — making the S election carries a deemed classification election with it)*.
> **That second election does not die with the first.** 🔑 **So what the buyer was left holding on
> 30 October is a C CORPORATION** — a company that pays its own tax at 21% — **not a disregarded
> single-member LLC.** ⚠️ **Verification status: stated from the regulation cited, which this session
> could NOT pull** (§9). **Confirm it before anyone tells the buyer's side anything.**

### ③ How the year gets SPLIT — and we do not get to choose

**When an S election dies mid-year, something has to decide which of the year's income and expenses
belong to each half. There are exactly two ways, and they do not give close answers.**

| | Method | What it would do here |
|---|---|---|
| **A · Pro rata, per day** — §1362(e)(2), the general default | **Ignore when things actually happened.** Take the WHOLE year's result and give each period its share of the *days* — 302/365 to us, 63/365 to the buyer | ⛔ **Would put `44,707 × 302 ÷ 365 = ` **36,990** on this return instead of 51,452** — a per-day slice of a full year, not a real result |
| **B · Normal accounting rules — "closing the books"** — §1362(e)(3) | **Cut the books on 29 October** and report what each period actually did | ✅ **51,452 — the *S period* column of this paper, exactly as already computed** |

🔑 **METHOD B IS MANDATORY HERE, AND THIS IS THE SINGLE MOST USEFUL SENTENCE IN THE SECTION.**
**§1362(e)(6)(D)**, and **Reg. §1.1362-3(b)(3)** under it, take method A away whenever **50% or more of
the stock changes hands during the S termination year.** **Here 100% changed hands.** ⛔ **So there is no
election to make, nothing for anyone to sign, and no consent to chase from a buyer nobody has spoken
to.** ✅ **The cut column IS the return, by law.**

## 6G · 🔴 NO PAYROLL, 42,517 OF DISTRIBUTIONS — the reasonable-compensation exposure, answered

**Lilian asked directly: does the fact that no payroll was run, while significant money was
distributed, create a problem for the company? 🛑 Yes. It is the single best-known exposure on an
S-corp return, and this one has an aggravating fact most cases do not.**

### What the rule actually is

**An S corporation pays no tax on its profit — it passes through to the owners, who pay income tax on
it but NOT social security or Medicare tax.** 🔑 **Wages, by contrast, carry both halves of FICA — about 15.3%**, of which the
**12.4% social security half stops at the wage base** while the **2.9% Medicare half has NO ceiling**
*(plus 0.9% more above the employee's Additional Medicare threshold, which the employer does not pay)*. So an owner who takes everything as a distribution and nothing as
salary avoids that 15.3% on the whole amount. **The IRS has said since Rev. Rul. 74-44 that a
shareholder who WORKS in the business must be paid reasonable compensation as wages first**, and
where none is paid it **recharacterises distributions as wages** and assesses the tax, plus the
payroll returns that were never filed and penalties on top.

### What this company's records show

| | |
|---|---|
| Wages paid in 2025 | 🔴 **zero** — ✅ **no wage account and no payroll-tax account in the 2025 ledger**, and **no payroll filings in Drive or Double**. ⚠️ **That is what those two searches found; the IRS transcript has not been pulled** *(method rule 1b)* |
| Distributions taken | **42,517** *(and potentially up to ~72,418 if §6D's plug is theirs)* |
| 🆕 **Payments to the owners for services, out of the company** | 🔴 **7,000** — booked as **`Contractors`**: Bogopolska **3,000** (31 Jan, check 241) and **2,000** (31 Mar, check 264); Makalendra **2,000** (31 Jan, check 242) |
| 2024 | **the same** — no payroll either. **A pattern, not a one-off year** |

🛑 **THE 7,000 IS THE PART THAT HURTS, AND IT IS NEW TODAY.** A defence built on *"the owners did not
really work in the business, it runs on contractors"* is contradicted by the company's own cheques:
**it paid them for services and called it contract labour.** ⚠️ **That is not a smaller version of
wages — it is the exact substitution the IRS looks for.**
⚠️ **And it forks the 1099 question rather than simply adding to it:** if the contractor
characterisation **stands**, a **1099-NEC was owed** and Schedule B 14a/14b covers it; if it **does
not**, a **W-2 was owed and a 1099 would have been the wrong form entirely.** 🔑 **Either way
something was not filed** — but do not write that a 1099 was owed to a shareholder-employee for
services, because under the theory being argued it was not.

### What actually happens if it is challenged

- The distributions, or a part of them, are **recharacterised as wages** → **employer and employee
  FICA**, roughly **15.3%** of whatever is recharacterised *(12.4% of it capped at the wage base, the
  2.9% Medicare part uncapped)*.
  ⚠️ **The NET cost is lower than 15.3%, and say so rather than overstating it:** recharacterised
  wages are **deductible by the company**, so ordinary income falls by the same amount and each
  shareholder's pass-through income falls with it. **The FICA is real; part of it is bought back in
  income tax.** ⛔ **What is NOT bought back is the penalties.**
- **Failure to file** Forms 941/940 and W-2, **failure to deposit**, and late-payment penalties — these
  are usually a large fraction of the tax itself.
- ⚠️ **It is the COMPANY's liability, and the company now belongs to the BUYER.** 🛑 **So this is not
  only a tax question, it is an indemnity question under the 2025 sale agreement** — read what the
  sellers warranted about taxes before anyone signs. *(The 2024 agreement, §6F, has exactly such a
  clause at its Article 12: the seller warranted all taxes paid in full for every year of the
  company's existence. If the 2025 agreement carries the same warranty, the sellers gave it.)*

### 🆕 And one cost that is NOT obvious — it reaches §199A

🔑 **W-2 wages of zero is what caps the shareholders' own §199A deduction** (§11D of the SOP). The
wage-and-UBIA limit **phases in above the taxable-income threshold** *(2025: $197,300 / $394,600)* and
**applies in FULL above $247,300 / $494,600** — **and both figures are zero here.** ⛔ **So the no-payroll position did not only expose the company to FICA — for a
shareholder above the threshold it also threw away up to 20% of the pass-through income as a
deduction.** ⚠️ **Below the threshold it costs nothing**, which is why this has to be worked out per
shareholder, on their 1040, not asserted here.
