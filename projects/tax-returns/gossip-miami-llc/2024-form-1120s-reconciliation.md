## 2 · 🛑 Read this before the tables — the direction of the difference is NOT established

**A difference is not an error until you know which side moved.** Three facts make that a live
question here, and none of them is resolved:

1. **The two records are on different bases.** The return is filed **CASH** (Schedule B line 1).
   The P&L export footer says **Accrual Basis**. A genuine cash/accrual difference is a *legitimate*
   reason for the small variances in §3D. ⚠️ **It is not a defence for §3's headline**: a
   cash-basis return should not be *missing cash receipts* — cash is the one thing the cash method
   is certain to have.
2. **The P&L was exported 17 months after the return was filed.** The books may have been
   rebuilt, re-categorized or corrected in between. The client file already records that **the firm
   produced a 2024 P&L in November 2025** and circulated a 2024 client copy in March 2026 — so the
   2024 column may be the firm's own reconstruction rather than what the outside CPA was handed.
3. **The outside preparer disclaimed verification in writing.** The engagement letter says the
   firm *"will depend on management to provide the information we need"* and *"will not audit or
   otherwise verify the data submitted."* So the return reflects **what management gave them**,
   which is not necessarily what the books now say.

🔑 **What this changes in practice:** the **cash-sales finding (§3A) survives all three** — an
84,823 account cannot be a basis difference, a re-categorization or a timing item, and it is
**absent from the return entirely**. Everything in §3D, by contrast, should be read as *"these two
records differ"*, not *"the return is wrong"*, until a ledger is looked at.

### 6C · 🔑 Why the goodwill clause in the 2025 agreement does NOT create company goodwill

**§2.1's allocation sentence is the one thing most likely to be misread, so it is worth being exact
about what it does.** It is a **statement between a buyer and a seller about what the buyer thinks
he is paying for.** It is *not* a §1060 asset-acquisition allocation, because **no assets were
acquired** — what was acquired was a membership interest.

Two further pieces of the same document confirm the reading rather than contradicting it:
- **§1.2(b)** sweeps in *"all tangible property, equipment, inventories, tenant improvements
  (regardless of whether they are accounted for as an asset on the books of Company …), goodwill,
  software, Intellectual Property …"*. ⚠️ **This is asset-purchase boilerplate sitting inside an
  interest-purchase agreement.** It describes what the buyer ends up controlling *by owning the
  company*; it does not make the company buy them. **The same template sloppiness elsewhere calls
  the salon "the tutoring center."**
- **§8.2** — *"Buyer shall not assume or agree to perform, pay or discharge, and Seller shall
  remain unconditionally liable for, all obligations … incurred in the operation of the Business
  prior to the Closing"*. ⚠️ **That is asset-deal language too**, and it is largely ineffective in
  an interest sale: the *company* keeps its own liabilities whoever owns it. It works only as an
  **indemnity between the individuals** — which is what it is.

⛔ **Do not read either clause as evidence of an asset transfer.** 🔑 **The operative documents are
§1.1 and Exhibit A, and both transfer the MEMBERSHIP INTEREST.**

### 6D · 🛑 The proposal in Julia's question — book $165,000 of goodwill, sell it in 2025 — and why it is wrong

The question was whether the proper treatment is to **book goodwill of $165,000** and then have
**that goodwill sold in 2025**. ⛔ **No. It would be wrong in three separate ways, and each one costs
money or creates exposure.**

| # | What breaks | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | 🔴 **It records a transaction the company never entered into** | The company paid nothing. Two individuals paid three other individuals. Debiting Goodwill 165,000 needs a credit, and there is no honest one: it is not a liability, and it is not paid-in capital — **the client file already settled that this money was "purchase price paid to the 2024 SELLERS, not money lent to this company"** |
| **2** | 🔴 **It manufactures a deduction the company is not entitled to** | §197 goodwill amortizes over **15 years — about 11,000 a year.** Deducting that on the 1120-S understates income for fifteen years on an asset the company never acquired |
| **3** | 🔴 **It taxes the owners' gain TWICE** | If the goodwill were the company's, its 2025 sale would be a **company-level** disposal — gain onto the K-1s. **But the sellers already have that gain personally**, from selling their interests. **The same economic gain would be taxed once through the company and again on their own Schedule D** |

| **2025 — $126,000 received for 100% of the interests** | ⛔ **Nowhere on the Form 1120-S.** A sale of a capital asset by the members | the selling members' **1040 · Form 8949 → Schedule D** |
| **The $12,000 broker fee** | a **selling expense** reducing amount realized | the sellers' Form 8949 — **not** the company |
| **Schedule 1.2(c)'s $42,030** | **descriptive, not a transaction** — it tells the buyer what physically comes with the company | **nowhere**, unless the *company* itself bought those items (§6G) |
| **The company's own asset basis** | 🔑 **carries over completely unchanged.** There is **no step-up** | — *(and there is no S-corporation equivalent of a §754 election to create one)* |

🔑 **The one-sentence version for the client:** *they did not buy a business's assets, they bought
the business — so the price is their personal cost of the shares, and the whole gain or loss lands
on their own tax return, not the company's.*

### 6F · 💰 They sold it for LESS than they paid — and nobody has claimed that loss

| | |
|---|---:|
| Paid, April 2024, for 100% | **165,000** |
| Received, October 2025, for 100% | **126,000** |
| Broker fee borne by the sellers | **(12,000)** |
| **Economic shortfall before basis adjustments** | 🔴 **(51,000)** |

🔴 **That is a capital loss on the selling members' personal returns, and it is worth real money to
them.** ⛔ **It is NOT simply 51,000 split two ways**, and it must not be reported that way:

1. **Stock basis is not the purchase price frozen in time.** It starts at cost and then moves —
   **up** by each year's share of income, **down** by losses, distributions and non-deductibles.
   So it needs the 2024 and 2025 K-1s and the distribution history to be right. 🔑 **Which is
   exactly why §3A's omitted 84,823 matters here:** if 2024 is restated from a loss to a profit,
   **basis rises** and the capital loss on the sale **falls**.
2. **The three sellers are not the two buyers.** Victoria Sapa signs the 2025 agreement as a seller
   *(and as "MGR" on the Assignment)*, but the filed 2024 return shows her holding nothing. **If she
   held no interest she has no amount realized**; if she did, there is a third K-1 and a third
   allocation of price. ⛔ **Open — FOLLOW-UPS row 59, and the Sunbiz check answers it.**
3. **Their purchase obligations were not symmetrical.** Bogopolska paid hers in full; Makalendra's
   80,000 runs in instalments to October 2027. ✅ **Basis is still the full contract price for both**
   — the 2024 agreement's §18(f) says the reversion remedy is *"in addition to, and do not exclude
   or limit"* other remedies, the buyers are **jointly and severally** liable, and late payments
   carry **8% per annum** — so the obligation is **recourse**, not a walk-away. _(This confirms the
   client file's own correction of an earlier non-recourse reading.)_

🛠️ **Action:** this belongs on the sellers' 2025 Form 8949, and the figure comes off their Form
7203s. **It is a separate engagement from the company return** — one return per request.

### 6G · 🟠 There IS a real bookkeeping gap here — but it is FIXED ASSETS, not goodwill

The company **has never carried a single fixed asset** — no Schedule L, no Form 4562, no
depreciation, in either year. Yet the salon demonstrably contains, per Schedule 1.2(c):

| Group | Stated |
|---|---:|
| Materials / build-out — flooring 3,800 · walls 6,000 · lighting 2,000 · additional 2,000 | **13,800** |
| Reception & office — desk, seating, laptop, showcase stock | ~5,575 |
| Nail bar · skin bar · make-up bar · coffee bar · sterilization · restrooms | ~18,755 |
| Consumables (manicure 2,000 · hair 1,000 · waxing 500) + marketing 400 | 3,900 |
| Decorations | ~4,145 |
| **TOTAL per the schedule** | **42,030** |

🔑 **The question the books cannot answer is WHO BOUGHT THEM AND WHEN**, and there are three
different right answers:
- **The old owners bought them before April 2024** → they came with the company; the company's basis
  is whatever it already was, and if it was expensed years ago it is **zero**. **Nothing to do.**
- **The company bought them** (before or after the change of hands) → **the build-out is leasehold
  improvements and the equipment is 5–7 year property**; they should have been **capitalized and
  depreciated**, and there should be a **Form 4562** on both returns. There is not.
- **The 2024 buyers bought them personally** → not the company's at all.

🛠️ **The document that answers it is the 2024 agreement's `Schedule 1` — the fixed-asset list —
which is NOT in the copy we hold.** ⛔ **Ask the client for it before deciding anything.**

> ⛔ **AND A HARD LIMIT: the 42,030 is NOT a tax basis and must never be used as one.** It is a
> **negotiated "as is" list prepared for a buyer** — §1.2(c) says the assets are purchased *"on an
> 'as is' basis in good working condition subject to normal wear and tear."* Several rows carry a
> quantity and no price; the consumables are round numbers; the decorations are estimates. 🔑 **It
> is the same trap as the Shopify inventory figure — it arrives looking authoritative and it is
> not.** _(See the [`shopify-year-end-inventory` skill](../../../.claude/skills/shopify-year-end-inventory/) for
> why that pattern keeps recurring.)_

- ⚠️ **We did not prepare 2024.** The client file records that whether JK filed, amended or merely
