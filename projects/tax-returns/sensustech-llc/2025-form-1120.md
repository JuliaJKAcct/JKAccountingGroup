## 0 · The year in one paragraph

2025 is the **first full year** of the company's two managed brokerage accounts at J.P. Morgan
Securities, opened **6 December 2024**. The shape of the investment return changed completely
between the two years: 2024 earned **interest** on cash and CDs and the portfolio lost value on
paper; 2025 earned essentially **no interest at all** — the fixed-income holdings are funds, which
pay dividends — and the portfolio **gained** substantially on paper while **realising a capital
loss**. Both facts matter for the return, and they pull in opposite directions on Schedule M-1.
The equity account (…1499) was **largely rebuilt in late November 2025**, which is relevant to the
dividends-received deduction (§4, decision D3).

### ① Why the 215,388 is not deductible — and it has nothing to do with the basis

🔴 **A corporation gets NO deduction for its own federal income tax — §275(a)(1).** Not in the year it
accrues, not in the year it is paid, not in any year.

**Cash basis does not help, and cannot.** The basis of accounting decides *when* a **deductible** item
is deducted. It has no power to make a **non-deductible** item deductible. There is no year in which
this 215,388 becomes a deduction.

**That is what Schedule M-1 line 2 is for.** The line is called *federal income tax per books* and it
exists precisely to take the tax back out of book income on the way to taxable income. The 2024 return
did the same thing — its page 1 carried no tax deduction either, and the Other Deductions statement has
no tax line in it.

ⓘ **The distinction that probably prompted the question: STATE income tax IS deductible** — page 1
line 17, taxes and licenses. **Federal is not.** This company files in Wyoming, which has no corporate
income tax, so nothing is lost.
