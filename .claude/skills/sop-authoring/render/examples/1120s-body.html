Standard Operating Procedure

Form 1120-SS-corporation return · from QuickBooks to a filed return

Written for someone preparing an S‑corporation return for the first time. It explains where each number comes from — not just which box it goes in, because the boxes are the easy part. The method at its centre is §3: reproduce last year's filed return from last year's books before you touch this year's.

◆

The process at a glance

Two things gate everything: the extension, which cannot be fixed later, and the map, which you build from last year before filling in anything this year. The rest is assembly in a fixed order.

§0A

Where every number comes from, and where it goes

Read this before anything else. Almost every mistake on a first return is a number taken from the wrong place, or a number that reached the right box by the wrong route. There are only four sources — everything else on the return is computed from them.

1The Profit & Losssource

Feeds page 1 income (lines 1a–5) and every deduction (lines 7–20). ⚠️ Not everything in the P&L’s income section belongs on page 1 — interest, dividends and capital gains go to Schedule K instead.

2The Balance Sheet at year endsource

Feeds 1125-A line 7, box F total assets, and the whole end column of Schedule L. Box F is read, never computed — but it must equal Schedule L line 15, and it has two free cross-checks: assets by category, and liabilities plus equity.

3LAST year’s FILED returnsource

Feeds 1125-A line 1, the beginning column of Schedule L, M-2 line 1 — and every convention (§3). ⚠️ It is a return, not a system. When the books and the prior return disagree, the prior return wins and the books get adjusted.

4The depreciation schedulesource

Feeds Form 4562 → page 1 line 14 → and, when the books never recorded it, M-1 line 6a as a book-to-tax difference.

The three places one number must appear twice

Where a beginner’s return most often fails to tie:
1 · Total assets — box F on page 1 and Schedule L line 15.
2 · Ordinary business income — page 1 line 22 and Schedule K line 1.
3 · Non-deductible expenses — Schedule K 16c, M-1 line 3b, and as a reduction in M-2 and in each shareholder’s basis.

§0B

What this return actually is

An S-corporation normally pays no federal income tax. It files an information return saying: this is what the business earned, and here is how it splits between the owners. Each owner then reports their share on their own 1040, through a Schedule K-1.

  1. The point of the exercise is page 1, line 22 — ordinary business income (loss) — plus the few items that must reach the owners separately rather than inside it. Line 22, not 21 — a new line 19 (Form 7205) pushed everything down from tax year 2023, so anything saying “line 21” describes 2022 or earlier. Everything else is bookkeeping around those two things.
  2. Some income and expenses never enter line 22. Interest, dividends, capital gains, §179 and charitable contributions are separately stated: they go on Schedule K and straight to the K-1s, because each owner's own treatment of them differs. Sweeping them into line 22 is the classic beginner error.
  3. A loss is not automatically deductible by the owner. It is limited by that owner's basis (§12). This is where a return that looks finished is often wrong.
The rule that governs everything below

The return is not a copy of the P&L. It is the P&L regrouped into the form's fixed categories, plus the adjustments where tax law and bookkeeping disagree. Every dollar in the books lands somewhere on the return — but almost never in the order QuickBooks shows it.

§1

Gather this before you start

You cannot prepare from the current year alone. Half of this list is about the prior year, and that is deliberate — see §3.

Documents to have in hand

  • The prior year's FILED return — the whole package: federal, any state, the K-1s. From the client's Tax Return Filed folder in Double.
  • This year's Profit & Loss — full year, accrual unless the client is on cash basis.
  • This year's Balance Sheet as of the last day of the year.
  • LAST year's P&L and Balance Sheet. Not optional — §3 is built on them.
  • The depreciation schedule — cost, date placed in service, method, life, depreciation taken so far.
  • Each shareholder's ownership percentage, and whether it changed during the year.
  • Whether shareholders put money in or took money out — and whether those were contributions or loans (§12).
  • Confirmation that Form 7004 was filed, if you are past 15 March.
Reading the prior-year return

Open it through the redactor (tools/redact-doc/), never the PDF directly, and one year only. The rule and its limits are in the double-mcp skill.

1A

Intake questions for the client

ask before you start, not when you are stuck
  1. Did the business buy or sell any equipment or vehicles this year?
  2. Did any shareholder put money in or take money out? Was it a loan or a contribution?
  3. Are there loans to or from the company — and did they change?
  4. Did the company take a physical inventory count at year end?
  5. Did anything change about the business itself — a new state, a new line of business, a closed location, a shareholder joining or leaving?
  6. Are there financial accounts outside the United States?
  7. Were Forms 1099 required, and were they filed?
§2

Gate: the extension

Check this first, because it cannot be fixed later.

  • The 1120-S for a calendar year is due 15 March of the following year — the next business day if that falls on a weekend or holiday.
  • Form 7004 extends it six months, to 15 September.
  • The firm files 7004 for most clients as a matter of course (Lilian, 2026-08-14) — but confirm it for this client, do not assume.
If no extension was filed and the deadline has passed

Stop and tell Lilian. The late-filing penalty on an S-corp is charged per shareholder, per month and climbs fast. Confirm the current rate against the IRS instructions rather than quoting one from memory. And note: the extension extends the filing, not the paying.

§3

Build the map from the prior year

This is the most important section here, and it is the step a beginner skips. Every firm, and every client, has conventions about how the books map onto the form — which account feeds which line, what gets grouped, what is netted. Those conventions are not written down anywhere. They are encoded in last year's filed return.

  1. Open last year's P&L and Balance Sheet, and last year's filed return.
  2. Reconstruct last year's page 1 from last year's P&L, on paper or in a spreadsheet.
  3. Compare, line by line, against what was actually filed.
  4. Every difference is information. Do not fix it and do not average it — find out why. That reason is the convention you must repeat.
  5. Only when your reconstruction reproduces the filed return do you have a trustworthy map.
What this catches — one real example

On a Shopify client, the ending-inventory line on the return did not equal the QuickBooks Inventory account. It equalled Total for Other Current Assets — the Inventory account plus a Shopify Clearing Account. Using Inventory alone broke the cost-of-goods schedule and drove purchases negative, which is impossible. Nobody could have guessed that convention; reproducing the prior year revealed it in ten minutes.

Two limits on what this proves

A number that reconciles is not the same as a number that is right. Reproducing the prior year proves your map is right — not the client's cost basis, inventory count or classifications.

And reproducing is not auditing. A filed return is closed. You are reading it as an answer key, not to find fault. If something looks wrong, raise it with Lilian — do not change this year's approach on your own initiative, because consistency between years is itself a tax position.

§4

Form 1125-A — cost of goods sold

Fill this in first. Its line 8 feeds page 1, and the form says so on its own face: "Enter here and on Form 1120-S, page 1, line 2." Skip it entirely if the client sells no goods.

Hover any line to see where its number comes from

Every line below opens on hover, on tap, or with the keyboard (Tab to it) to show the formula or the source. calculated means it falls out of other numbers; read means you look it up and the risk is reading the wrong source. When you print, every one of them opens automatically — paper has no hover.

1Inventory at beginning of yearread
= line 7 of LAST year’s Form 1125-A

The prior return governs — never a system, never a recalculation. If the books disagree with it, the books are what get adjusted.

2Purchasescalculated
line 2 = line 8 + line 7 − line 1

Usually the figure you solve for, because nobody adds up a year of purchase invoices. It is derived, not measured — which is why a wrong ending inventory shows up here as an impossible number. If it comes out negative, your map is wrong.

3Cost of laborread

Direct production labour only. Most retail and e-commerce clients have none.

4Additional §263A costsread

Almost always blank for a small business taxpayer.

5Other costsread

Whatever the firm’s convention puts in cost of sales beyond purchases. Check the prior year before adding anything here.

6Totalcalculated
line 6 = line 1 + line 2 + line 3 + line 4 + line 5

Straight arithmetic.

7Inventory at end of yearread
= the balance-sheet subtotal the prior return used

⚠️ Not automatically the account called Inventory. On some clients it is a subtotal covering more than that one account — see 4B. Read it off the section the prior return used, not off the account name.

8Cost of goods soldcalculated
line 8 = line 6 − line 7 → carry to page 1, line 2

The form says so on its own face: “Enter here and on Form 1120-S, page 1, line 2.”

4A

The identity, and how to use it

solve for the one you don't know
line 1  +  line 2  −  line 7  =  line 8
  • Purchases recorded to an asset account (perpetual): you know 1, 2 and 7 → line 8 falls out.
  • Purchases expensed, cost of sales estimated (periodic): you know 1, 7 and 8 → line 2 falls out.
If the figure you solve for comes out NEGATIVE, your map is wrong

Negative purchases are not a thing. Go back to §3 — you are almost certainly reading the wrong account into line 7.

4B

The line 7 trap

the one that cost a day

"Inventory at end of year" is not automatically the account called Inventory. On some clients the return's inventory line is a subtotal covering more than that one account — the Shopify example in §3 is Inventory plus a clearing account, which QuickBooks already presents as one line: Total for Other Current Assets.

Read line 7 off the balance-sheet section the PRIOR RETURN used. Do not decide it from the account's name.

4C

Line 9 — the checkboxes

  • 9a — the valuation method. Copy what was ticked last year. Changing it is a change of accounting method — a formal election, not a preference.
  • 9b–9d — subnormal goods, LIFO. Normally all "no".
  • 9e — do the §263A rules apply? Normally no for a small business taxpayer.
  • 9f — did quantities, cost or valuation change? A "yes" here is a problem to escalate, not a box to tick.
§5

Form 1120-S, page 1

Header boxes A to I
  • A · B · D · E — S election date, activity code, EIN, date incorporated. All copied from the prior return.
  • F · Total assets — from the year-end balance sheet.
  • H(1) · Final return — 🛑 tick only if this is the last year the company exists. A company closing next year is not a final return this year.
  • I — number of shareholders. Count the K-1s.
5B

Income — lines 1 to 6

1aGross receipts or salescalculated
= sales revenue − discounts given + shipping income charged

Discounts given at the point of sale reduce receipts — you never earned them. Shipping the customer paid you is revenue.

1bReturns and allowancesread

The refunds-to-customers account.

1cBalancecalculated
line 1c = line 1a − line 1b

✅ Must equal the P&L’s total income. Your first tie-out check.

2Cost of goods soldread
= Form 1125-A, line 8

Nothing to compute here — it was computed on the other form.

3Gross profitcalculated
line 3 = line 1c − line 2

✅ Must equal the P&L’s gross profit.

4Net gain (loss), Form 4797read

Gain or loss on selling business equipment. Zero in most years.

5Other income (loss)read

⚠️ Trade-or-business income only. Interest, dividends and capital gains are portfolio income and go on Schedule K. Bank interest here understates Schedule K and overstates ordinary income.

6Total incomecalculated
line 6 = line 3 + line 4 + line 5
Line 5 is NOT "everything else on the P&L"

Interest income, dividends and capital gains are portfolio income and go on Schedule K, not here. Bank interest on line 5 understates Schedule K and overstates ordinary income.

5C

Deductions — lines 7 to 21

work top-down; line 19 is the remainder

The mapping rule, which is the whole skill: lines 7 to 19 are named categories the IRS wants to see separately. Line 20 is everything left over. So you place each expense that has its own line first, then group what remains.

5D

Depreciation — line 14

  • A full year for an asset placed in service last year is larger than its first part-year. A beginner expecting "the same as last year" will understate the deduction.
  • No new assets means no new elections — no §179 or bonus decisions. Just continue the schedule.
  • ⚠️ If the books recorded no depreciation at all, the deduction still belongs on the return — it becomes a book-to-tax difference on Schedule M-1.
§6

Schedule B — the questions

⚠️ The question numbers move between tax years. Find each one by its wording, not its number.

  1. Accounting method — accrual or cash. Read it off the footer of the QuickBooks reports; they say which basis they were run on.
  2. "Are total receipts AND total assets both under $250,000?" If yes, Schedules L and M-1 are not required. The firm completes them anyway when the prior year did — they give continuity, and the balance sheet is the best proof nothing was mapped wrong.
  3. Were Forms 1099 required, and were they filed? Answer honestly; the firm usually knows because it prepares them.
Being excused from Schedule M-1 does not remove the adjustment

A non-deductible expense still changes line 22 whether or not you disclose the reconciliation.

§8

Schedules L · M-1 · M-2

8A

Schedule L — the balance sheet

  1. 🔑 The beginning column is COPIED from last year's filed return. Never recalculated. If it does not match, stop — that is a mapping change or an error, and both need Lilian.
  2. The end column comes from the year-end balance sheet; total assets must agree to the dollar.
  3. 🔑 The heading says "per Books" and it means it. If the books did not record something, Schedule L shows the books' figure and the difference goes on Schedule M-1. Do not "correct" it to tax figures.
  4. Equity mapping is a convention, not a calculation. How capital accounts split between capital stock, paid-in capital and retained earnings is whatever the prior return did. Repeat it, or the two years cannot be compared.
8B

Schedule M-1 — where books and return disagree

the bridge

Book net income in at the top, taxable ordinary income out at the bottom. If you understand this schedule, you understand why the return is not a copy of the P&L.

  • Line 1 — net income per books, straight off the P&L.
  • Line 3b — expenses in the books the return does not allow. The common one: the 50% of meals that is not deductible.
  • Line 6a — deductions on the return the books never recorded. The common one: depreciation.
  • Line 8 — the result. ⚠️ Must equal SCHEDULE K LINE 18, not page 1 line 22. They are equal only when there are no separately stated items at all — the moment there is bank interest they differ, and forcing them to agree misstates the return.
A quick sanity check on the prior year

The gap between last year's book net income and last year's ordinary income is last year's M-1. If that gap is roughly half the prior year's meals, you have just confirmed both the convention and your understanding of it.

8C

Schedule M-2 — the AAA

  • Beginning balance = last year's ending balance. Copy it from the filed return.
  • Add the year's income or subtract the year's loss; subtract non-deductible expenses (the same figure as Schedule K 16c); subtract distributions.
  • AAA can go negative from losses. It cannot be driven negative by distributions.
  • ⚠️ Shareholder capital contributions do NOT increase AAA. They increase basis (§12). Two different accounts that beginners merge.
§12

Form 7203 — basis, and why a loss may not be deductible

This is the part of an S-corp return that beginners omit, and it is often the part that changes the answer.

   what they put IN  (stock purchased + capital contributed)
 + their share of income in profitable years
 − their share of losses already deducted
 − distributions they received
 = basis

A loss beyond basis is suspended — carried forward until basis is restored, not deducted now. Form 7203 is required when a shareholder claims a loss, receives a distribution, disposes of stock, or receives a loan repayment from the company.

12A

Two questions to settle before you compute it

  1. Was money the shareholder put in a CAPITAL CONTRIBUTION or a LOAN? Both support losses, but they are tracked separately — stock basis vs debt basis — and restored in a different order. The books may not distinguish them. Ask.
  2. Are contributions proportionate to ownership? If one owner funded the business and the other did not, their basis is very different at identical ownership percentages — so the same loss allocation can be fully deductible for one and partly suspended for the other. Check each shareholder separately. Never assume symmetry.
§14

Before you file — the tie-out checks

Do not file until every one of these passes. A check that fails is a mapping error, not a rounding difference.

Every one must tie

  • 1125-A: line 6 − line 7 = line 8, and line 8 equals page 1 line 2
  • Page 1: 1a − 1b = 1c, and 1c equals the P&L's total income
  • Page 1: 1c − 2 = 3, and 3 equals the P&L's gross profit
  • Page 1: 6 − 21 = 22
  • Line 20 equals total expenses less lines 7–19 less non-deductible amounts
  • Schedule L: the beginning column matches last year's filed ending column exactly
  • Schedule L: total assets = total liabilities and equity, and equals the year-end balance sheet
  • Schedule M-1 line 8 equals Schedule K line 18
  • Schedule M-2 beginning balance matches last year's ending balance
  • The K-1 percentages add to 100%, and each Schedule K line equals the sum across all K-1s
  • A Form 7203 exists for every shareholder who needs one
  • Form 8879-CORP signed before e-filing — not 8879-S, superseded from TY2022
§15

Common pitfalls

Each of these has bitten a real return.

  1. Treating the P&L's net income as ordinary business income. The difference is Schedule M-1 — usually meals and unrecorded depreciation.
  2. Reading "inventory" as the account called Inventory. Check what the prior return used (§4B).
  3. Leaving advertising, rent or interest inside "other deductions" (line 20). Arithmetically fine, but it no longer matches the prior year and nobody can compare them.
  4. Putting bank interest on page 1 line 5 instead of Schedule K.
  5. Recalculating the Schedule L beginning column instead of copying it.
  6. Correcting the books on Schedule L — it is "per books" on purpose.
  7. Skipping Form 7203 because the loss "obviously" flows through. It may not.
  8. Ticking "final return" a year early because the client is winding down.
  9. Zero officer compensation without a decision. A shareholder who works in the business is expected to take a reasonable salary. Not doing so may be defensible in a heavy loss year — but it must be a documented decision, not an oversight.
  10. Changing a method because it looks wrong. Consistency with the prior year is itself a tax position. Raise it with Lilian; do not fix it silently.

   sum of the K-1s for any line  MUST EQUAL  that Schedule K line
   the percentages               MUST TOTAL  100%

FORM 7203 — BASIS
basis  =  stock purchased + capital contributed
          + their share of income in profitable years
          − their share of losses already deducted
          − distributions received

deductible loss  =  the LESSER of (their share of the loss) and (their basis)
suspended loss   =  their share of the loss − the deductible amount
Basis is per shareholder, never per company

Two owners at the same ownership percentage can have very different basis — so the same loss allocation is fully deductible for one and partly suspended for the other. Compute it separately for each. Never assume symmetry.
